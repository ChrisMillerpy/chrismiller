Backprojection by hand

How a CT scanner turns shadows into slices.

A CT scanner never photographs a slice of your body. It fires X-rays through you from hundreds of directions and records how much each beam is dimmed.Modern scanners use a fan or cone of rays and a rotating gantry. Parallel beams keep the maths simple, so we start there. The picture you see afterwards is a calculation.

What the scanner measures

After taking logs, each detector reading is a line integral of the attenuation $f$ along the beam.Beer–Lambert: $I = I_0 \exp\!\left(-\int f\,dt\right)$, so $-\log(I/I_0)$ is the integral. Collect every line at angle $\theta$ and offset $s$ and you get the Radon transform,

$$p_\theta(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(s\cos\theta - t\sin\theta,\; s\sin\theta + t\cos\theta)\,dt.$$
(1)

Stacking the projections for $\theta \in [0,\pi)$ gives the sinogram, named for the sine curve each point of the object traces.

Smearing it back

The obvious inverse is to smear each projection back along the direction it came from:

$$b(x,y) = \int_0^{\pi} p_\theta(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta)\,d\theta.$$
(2)

With the filter off in Figure 1 the shapes appear, but blurred. In fact $b = f * \tfrac{1}{r}$.A point source projects to a spike at every angle; smearing the spikes back gives spokes whose density falls off like $1/r$.

The fix is a filter

By the Fourier slice theorem, the 1-D transform of $p_\theta$ is a slice of the 2-D transform of $f$. Backprojection over-samples low frequencies, and weighting each slice by $|\omega|$ corrects it:

$$f(x,y) = \int_0^{\pi} (p_\theta * h)(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta)\,d\theta, \qquad \hat h(\omega) = |\omega|.$$
(3)

With 180 angles the phantom returns almost exactly; with 12, streaks appear.The discrete Ram-Lak kernel below is from Kak & Slaney, Principles of Computerized Tomographic Imaging, ch. 3.

def ramp_filter(p, ds):
    n = np.arange(-len(p) + 1, len(p))
    h = np.where(n == 0, 1 / (4 * ds**2),
                 np.where(n % 2, -1 / (np.pi * n * ds)**2, 0))
    return ds * np.convolve(p, h)[len(p) - 1 : 2 * len(p) - 1]
